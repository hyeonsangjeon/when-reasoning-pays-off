Invisible reasoning tokens: the part of the bill the answer never shows

After a reasoning migration, or after someone nudges the effort dial up, the answers can still look short and ordinary. The visible text gives no hint that spending has changed. Yet finance and telemetry see a larger bill for the same workload. If the answer did not get longer, where did the cost go?

Why we tested this

Teams often estimate the cost of a request from the part they can see: the length of the answer. A short reply feels cheap and a long one expensive; for most of the pre-reasoning era, that intuition was close enough to be useful.

We checked whether visible answer length explains the spend or whether a reasoning model moves the cost into hidden reasoning tokens the user never reads. The chart shows which component carries the change.

We split each billed request into input, cached input, visible output, and hidden reasoning, then read that breakdown next to cost, quality, and latency: first on the short-factual slice, then on the multi-step slice. On short-factual work, the hidden reasoning channel barely opened: visible output moved only about 14.0 to 14.8 tokens across the effort ladder, and hidden reasoning stayed near zero, from 0 at minimal effort to under one token at its peak. That is why the bill stayed flat. The invisible channel opened only when the task demanded multi-step resolution, climbing from 0 to about 110 reasoning tokens per request.

Question

If the answer text stays short, where did the extra cost come from?

Evidence

benchmark-01 and benchmark-02 token composition, read next to cost, quality, and latency as one evidence chain.

Finding

On short-factual work the hidden reasoning channel stayed near zero, so the bill stayed flat; on multi-step work the same channel scaled from 0 to about 110 tokens per request.

Decision

Track reasoning tokens in telemetry even when the visible answer stays short. Treat the hidden-token budget as part of the bill the response never shows.

When the hidden channel stays shut — and when it opens

The useful surprise in benchmark-01 is what did not happen. Turning the effort dial up did not quietly inflate a hidden budget. Minimal effort averaged about 14.0 visible output tokens and 0 reasoning tokens. Extra-high effort averaged about 14.8 visible output tokens and still under one reasoning token. The hidden channel stayed shut, so the bill stayed flat and every gpt-5.2 cell landed at or below the gpt-4o baseline. On a short factual answer, the model simply had nothing to deliberate about.

The multi-step slice is where the same decomposition tells the opposite story. There, hidden reasoning climbed from 0 tokens at minimal effort to about 110 at xhigh, while visible output rose alongside it from about 6 to 117 tokens. That is the case the intuition misses: the text a reader sees becomes a poor proxy for the computation that was purchased, because a growing share of the work never reaches the answer. Only the token decomposition, rather than answer length, shows whether a hidden budget is being spent.

How to read the chart

The bars are aggregate effort cells, not a histogram of individual requests. The X-axis is the reasoning-effort setting. The Y-axis is mean reasoning tokens per request. The small label under each bar gives mean visible output tokens for the same cell.

Read the chart from bottom to top: first ask whether visible output changed enough to explain the bill; then compare the hidden reasoning bar. On the short-factual control both bars stay low, so the flat bill needs no hidden explanation. On the multi-step slice the hidden bar is the one that climbs.

What the source charts add

Token composition explains the mechanism, but it is not enough by itself. The cost chart shows whether the bill moved. The quality chart asks whether any spend bought a better answer. The latency chart shows the user-facing wait. Together they make the hidden surface legible: on the short-factual control the internal tokens, the spend, and the quality all stay flat together; on multi-step work the internal tokens are what move the bill.

What this means

Reasoning tokens are the point of a reasoning model, but they need a job. If the task only needs a short factual answer, internal reasoning can become a silent budget leak. If the task needs multi-step resolution, those same tokens may be exactly what earns the quality gain.

This is why the overview essay keeps cost, quality, latency, and token composition together. The bill is not fully visible in the answer.

Sources and evidence boundary

Every number above traces to a public artifact in this repository, and every cost claim is priced against a dated snapshot. The accounting habit this essay recommends — keep the reasoning-token budget visible in telemetry — is its own operating inference. What this evidence does and does not prove: it shows that the hidden reasoning channel is task-dependent — near zero on the short-factual control, and climbing to nearly match visible output on the multi-step slice. It does not prove the same shape for tool-agent or synthesis work; each topic carries its own measurement. The point is the habit, not the magnitude: only the token decomposition tells you which case you are in.

The practical rule

  1. Log reasoning tokens per request next to visible output, not just total tokens.
  2. Alert on the hidden-token budget, since a short answer can still carry a large internal cost.
  3. Promote a higher effort only after the paired quality chart moves on this workload shape.

Measure reasoning tokens in telemetry even when the visible answer stays short — the hidden-token budget is the part of the bill the response does not show.
