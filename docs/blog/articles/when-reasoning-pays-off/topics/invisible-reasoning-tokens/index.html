Invisible reasoning tokens: the part of the bill the answer does not show

After a reasoning migration — or after someone nudges the effort dial up — the answers can still look short and ordinary. Nothing about the visible text says you are spending more. Yet finance and telemetry see a larger bill for the same workload. The natural question writes itself: if the answer did not get longer, where did the cost go?

Why we tested this

The habit is almost universal. When teams reason about what a request costs, they reach for the thing they can see — the length of the answer. A short reply feels cheap, a long one feels expensive, and for most of the pre-reasoning era that intuition was close enough to be useful.

That gives us a clean thing to check. Maybe visible answer length really does explain the spend, and the bigger bill is hiding in slightly longer replies nobody noticed. Or maybe a reasoning model breaks the intuition, and the cost has moved into hidden reasoning tokens the user never reads. The chart can show which one carries the change.

So we decomposed it. We split each billed request into input, cached input, visible output, and hidden reasoning, and read that decomposition next to cost, quality, and latency on the same short-factual slice. The result settles the question: visible output moved only about 12.7 to 17.9 tokens across the effort ladder, while hidden reasoning moved about 3.7 to 311.5 tokens. Cost and latency grew alongside the hidden channel, and the paired quality chart did not lift to match. The one-pager below states that in a single frame; the sections after it show the measured detail.

The answer was short; the internal work was not

The useful surprise in benchmark-01 is not that extra-high effort cost more. That part is expected. The useful surprise is how little of the increase was visible in the final answer. Minimal effort averaged 12.7 visible output tokens and 3.7 reasoning tokens. Extra-high effort averaged 17.9 visible output tokens and 311.5 reasoning tokens.

That means the text a reader sees is a poor proxy for the computation that was purchased. A short answer can hide a large reasoning budget, and a plausible answer can still be a bad economic trade if the paired quality chart does not move.

How to read the chart

The bars are aggregate effort cells, not a histogram of individual requests. The X-axis is the reasoning-effort setting. The Y-axis is mean reasoning tokens per request. The small label under each bar gives mean visible output tokens for the same cell.

Read the chart from bottom to top: first ask whether visible output changed enough to explain the bill; then compare the hidden reasoning bar. In this slice, the explanation is not answer length. It is internal work.

What the source charts add

Token composition explains the mechanism, but it is not enough by itself. The cost chart shows the bill growing. The quality chart asks whether that bill bought a better answer. The latency chart shows the user-facing wait. Together they make the hidden surface legible: more internal tokens, more spend, more latency, and no matching quality lift in this short factual slice.

What this means

The lesson is not that reasoning tokens are bad. They are the point of a reasoning model. The lesson is that they need a job. If the task only needs a short factual answer, internal reasoning can become a silent budget leak. If the task needs multi-step resolution, those same tokens may be exactly what earns the quality gain.

This is why the overview essay keeps cost, quality, latency, and token composition together. The bill is not fully visible in the answer.

What this slice does and does not prove: it shows that for this short-factual slice, internal reasoning grew across the effort ladder while visible output barely moved. It does not prove that the same hidden-token shape will appear in multi-step, tool-agent, or synthesis work; each topic carries its own measurement.

The practical rule

  1. Log reasoning tokens per request next to visible output, not just total tokens.
  2. Alert on the hidden-token budget, since a short answer can still carry a large internal cost.
  3. Promote a higher effort only after the paired quality chart moves on this workload shape.

The practical rule: measure reasoning tokens in telemetry even when the visible answer stays short — the hidden-token budget is the part of the bill the response does not show.
