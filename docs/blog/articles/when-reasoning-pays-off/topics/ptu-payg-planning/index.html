PTU/PAYG crossover: a planning line, not a capacity result

The crossover chart is useful precisely because it is limited. It turns the public cost and token-shape snapshot into a planning question: at what request rate would fixed PTU spend begin to look cheaper than PAYG for this measured workload shape? It does not prove how a live PTU target will behave under load.

Question

What can a modeled PTU/PAYG crossover tell us, and what must it not claim?

Evidence

A break-even calculation that combines two dated pricing snapshots — pricing/azure-openai-payg-2026-05.yaml and pricing/azure-openai-ptu-2026-05.yaml — with the measured per-request cost and token-shape throughput factor from benchmark-01, -02, and -03. The output is the modeled_break_even_rpm series in the crossover chart data, not a measured throughput.

Finding

At this pricing snapshot, the modeled break-even lands wherever the per-request bill and token shape put it: benchmark-01 extra-high near 149 rpm, benchmark-02 none near 2,680 rpm, benchmark-03 low near 564 rpm — each scaled by the 50 PTU floor at $2.00/PTU-hour. These are calculated figures that move with the pricing snapshot and the token-per-request mix; they are not measured saturation points.

Decision

Read the crossover as a planning line that tells the team when a capacity experiment is worth running on this workload shape — not as a verdict that a deployment needs PTU. Re-derive it when pricing, token mix, or region changes.

The model answers a narrow question

The crossover calculation combines mean cost per request, token-shape throughput factor, a public pricing snapshot, and a 50 PTU floor. The output is a modeled break-even RPM. For example, benchmark-01 extra-high effort lands around 149 rpm, benchmark-02 none around 2,680 rpm, and benchmark-03 low around 564 rpm.

Those numbers are useful because they expose how workload shape changes the planning conversation. They are not live-load measurements. They do not say where 429 begins, how cache behaves near saturation, or what a particular serving setup will do at p95 latency.

How to read the chart

The X-axis is a set of selected benchmark rows. The Y-axis is modeled break-even RPM. A taller bar means PAYG can rise to a higher request rate before the modeled PTU floor looks cheaper. A shorter bar means the workload reaches the modeled crossover earlier.

The bars are not a continuous capacity curve. They are planning markers derived from public aggregate data and a pricing snapshot.

Why the distinction matters

Without the distinction, a crossover chart can be misread as a promise. It is tempting to say that a row with a 149 rpm crossover “needs PTU” or that a row with a 2,680 rpm crossover “does not need PTU.” The evidence is more careful than that. The chart says what the economics look like under the modeled assumptions. A live system still needs latency, cache, retry, and saturation evidence.

That caution is not weakness. It is what makes the chart usable: it tells the team when to run a capacity experiment, not what the capacity experiment will find.

The story to keep

PAYG and PTU answer different questions. PAYG asks how much each completed request costs. PTU asks what fixed capacity can carry at an acceptable quality and latency level. The crossover line connects those worlds, but it does not collapse them into one measurement.

In this project, the line is therefore labeled as a modeled hypothesis. It belongs beside the measured charts, not above them.

Sources and evidence boundary

Every break-even figure above is a calculation, not a measurement. It is produced by pricing a measured per-request cost and token shape against two dated pricing snapshots. The two tiers below mark where the documented inputs end and the modeled output begins. The 149 rpm crossover with a 50 PTU floor is derived arithmetic — it is not a measured throughput, a 429 onset, or a saturation point.

  • Tier 1 — methodology contract: this repository, docs/05-methodology.md (v1.0). N = 20 authored samples per cell, R = 3 repeats, public aggregate slice only. The per-request cost inputs inherit this frozen stance; error bars are standard deviation, not confidence intervals, and no p-values are claimed at N = 20.
  • Tier 1 — PAYG pricing snapshot: this repository, pricing/azure-openai-payg-2026-05.yaml. accessed_date: 2026-05-19; source_url: https://azure.microsoft.com/en-us/pricing/details/azure-openai/; archive_url: https://web.archive.org/web/20260519042011/https://azure.microsoft.com/en-us/pricing/details/azure-openai/. The per-token rates here (gpt-5.2 input $1.75/1M, cached input $0.175/1M, output and reasoning $14.00/1M; gpt-4o input $2.50/1M, cached $1.25/1M, output $10.00/1M) set the per-request bill that feeds the formula.
  • Tier 1 — PTU pricing snapshot: this repository, pricing/azure-openai-ptu-2026-05.yaml. accessed_date: 2026-05-19; source_url: https://learn.microsoft.com/en-us/azure/foundry/openai/how-to/provisioned-throughput-onboarding; archive_url: https://web.archive.org/web/20260519041942/https://learn.microsoft.com/en-us/azure/foundry/openai/how-to/provisioned-throughput-onboarding (region eastus2). The PTU side of the formula — $2.00 per PTU-hour, a 50 PTU minimum (the floor), and the Input TPM per PTU used for the throughput factor (gpt-4o 2,500; gpt-5.2 3,400) — comes from this snapshot. Both the PAYG and PTU prices feed the break-even: a PAYG bill per request on one side, a fixed $100/hour PTU floor (50 × $2.00) on the other.
  • Tier 2 — measured per-request inputs: this repository, benchmarks/01-short-factual/analysis.json, benchmarks/02-multi-step-reasoning/analysis.json, and benchmarks/03-tool-using-agent/analysis.json. These supply the mean cost per request and the per-cell token-per-request distribution that becomes the throughput-gain factor relative to the gpt-4o baseline.
  • Tier 2 — modeled crossover output (CALCULATION, not measurement): this repository, docs/blog/data/chart-data/ptu-payg-crossover/benchmark-01/crossover.json, benchmark-02/crossover.json, and benchmark-03/crossover.json. The modeled_break_even_rpm series rendered by the chart is computed as (min_ptu × ptu_hourly_rate ÷ (60 × mean_usd_per_request)) × throughput_gain_factor. The 149 rpm figure for benchmark-01 extra-high, the 50 PTU floor, and the $2.00 PTU-hour rate are all calculated or quoted inputs — labeled MODELED, not measured throughput.
  • Evidence dashboard: the rendered PTU/PAYG crossover chart family is the auditable form of the same JSON artifacts.

What this slice does and does not prove: it shows where the modeled break-even sits at this pricing snapshot, for a workload with the measured token-per-request distribution of benchmark-01, -02, and -03. It does not prove that the crossover holds for a different pricing snapshot, for a different workload shape, cache-hit ratio, or reasoning mix, or for a different deployment region. It is a planning aid, not an SLA, and not a statement about live capacity.

What the evidence lets us say

The crossover is valuable when it stays humble: at this pricing snapshot, for this token distribution, it converts evidence into a planning question and leaves live capacity to live measurement.

The practical rule

  1. Treat the break-even RPM as price-only arithmetic, derived from the dated PAYG and PTU snapshots — re-derive it when either snapshot moves.
  2. Match it to the measured token shape of the slice you are planning, not to a generic workload.
  3. Use a low crossover as a trigger to run a capacity experiment, not as proof a deployment needs PTU.
  4. Before committing PTU, weigh the non-price factors the chart cannot see: latency targets, admission concurrency, and burst tolerance.

The practical rule: the crossover marks only the price-only break-even at this snapshot and token shape — decide PTU versus PAYG by pairing it with the non-price factors it cannot model: latency, admission, and burst.
