Short factual work: when extra reasoning buys cost, not quality

When a team first adopts reasoning models, short factual questions look like the harmless place to turn the effort dial up. The answer is short, mistakes are easy to spot, and “let it think a little longer” sounds like cheap insurance. So the instinct is to raise reasoning effort here first. That instinct is a hypothesis to test, not a policy to ship, and this essay tests it with data.

Why we tested this

The pattern is familiar. After a migration, the team lands on a reasoning model, the configuration exposes a reasoning-effort dial, and short factual questions look like the safest place to turn it. The output is brief, errors are obvious, and “let it think a bit more” sounds like cheap insurance. Raising the default here feels almost free.

That gives us a clean hypothesis to check: on short factual work, does higher reasoning effort actually make the answer safer? If it does, the higher default earns its place. If it does not, the team is paying for thinking that never reaches the reader.

So we measured it. We held the workload shape fixed, using the same short-factual slice, and walked the effort ladder one step at a time. At each level we compared cost per request, latency, visible answer text, hidden reasoning tokens, and judge score. In this slice, the result was the kind that should move a default: effort bought extra cost, latency, and hidden tokens, but not a better answer. The one-pager below states that in a single frame, and the sections after it show the measured detail.

Question

If the task mostly asks for short factual answers, does higher reasoning effort improve quality enough to justify the bill?

Evidence

benchmark-01 cost, quality, latency, and token-shape charts, paired with the public data tables.

Finding

On this slice, cost per request stayed essentially flat: about 1.02x from none to xhigh, with every gpt-5.2 tier below the gpt-4o baseline. The mean judge score held around 1.95.

Decision

Default to the lowest effort that already answers correctly. Escalate only after an evaluator shows a real quality lift on this workload shape.

What the data showed

The hypothesis predicted a safer answer at higher effort. The data did not deliver one. The cost bars stay essentially flat — $0.000587 at none effort and $0.000598 at xhigh, about a 1.02x span, with every gpt-5.2 tier below the gpt-4o baseline of $0.000665 — and the quality bars are just as flat: the mean judge score is about 1.95 across the ladder, at or above the baseline's 1.90. Latency shows no trend either (roughly 1.4s to 1.7s with no climb), and the token-shape throughput factor holds around 0.99.

Neither the cost bars nor the quality bars moved with effort. That is the whole story the chart is trying to tell. Spending more on reasoning bought neither a cheaper bill nor a better answer, and gpt-5.2 at its lowest setting already matched or beat the gpt-4o baseline. For this workload shape, higher effort should stay experimental rather than become the default.

Why the hypothesis can fail

The safe-looking default can still disappoint, because short factual work rarely needs a long internal search. When the answer is a single fact, the bottleneck tends to be retrieval, a stable prompt, or simply the lowest effort setting that already lands the correct answer — not extra deliberation. Past that point more thinking has nothing left to find, so the effort ladder keeps adding hidden tokens without adding accuracy.

That is the shape the slice shows. The judge score does not climb with effort; it holds around 1.95 while cost, latency, and reasoning tokens stay essentially flat. There is almost no hidden thinking to pay for here — which is exactly why a higher default on this slice buys nothing the cheaper setting had not already delivered.

How to read the chart

The bars are aggregate bar charts, not frequency histograms. Each bar stands for one effort cell in benchmark-01, so the comparison is about how the same workload shape changes as effort rises.

X-axis

The bars move left to right across reasoning effort levels: baseline, none, low, medium, high, and xhigh. Treat this as the control knob the operator can change.

Y-axis

In the cost chart, bar height means mean USD per request. In the quality chart, bar height means mean judge score. Read them as a pair, not separately.

Bars and table

Each bar is an aggregate cell. The table below the chart shows the exact value, standard deviation where available, and sample count. Error bars are standard deviation, not confidence intervals.

What the source charts contribute

Each source chart answers a different part of the decision. The cost chart says what the setting costs. The quality chart says whether the spend bought a better answer. Latency and token composition explain the operational side effects. The throughput view translates the same token shape into planning pressure.

What happened

The cost curve stayed flat. None effort averaged $0.000587 per request; xhigh effort averaged $0.000598 per request, about a 1.02x span, and every gpt-5.2 tier sat below the gpt-4o baseline of $0.000665. The paired quality was flat too: the mean judge score held around 1.95, at or above the baseline's 1.90.

That is not a universal claim that reasoning is bad. It is a workload shape claim. In this slice, there was almost no added internal computation to pay for, and raising effort bought neither a cheaper bill nor a better answer. The dashboard makes that visible because cost and quality are kept side by side.

The trap is the opposite intuition: higher effort must be safer. If a reviewer samples only a few answers, xhigh effort can feel stronger because the model seems to have thought harder. The chart keeps that feeling from becoming policy: the quality delta is flat, so the cheaper setting keeps the same answer without a slower response or a larger bill.

What we learned while benchmarking

More thinking is not a default

Effort is a treatment. It should be applied to a slice, measured, and promoted only if the evaluator moves. Treating it as a global default hides the bill until production traffic arrives.

Cost must be paired with quality

A cost-only chart can make the change look obvious but incomplete. A quality-only chart can hide the economic cost. The useful view is the pair: what did we buy, and did it improve the answer?

Short factual work needs a low-effort baseline

The lowest acceptable effort level is the baseline to beat. If a higher level does not beat it on quality, the operator should keep the cheaper setting and spend optimization time elsewhere.

When to revisit the decision

Do not freeze this conclusion forever. Re-test if the task changes from short recall into synthesis, if the judge rubric starts rewarding multi-hop justification, if the prompt begins carrying tool outputs, or if a new model changes the cost-quality shape. The lesson is not “never use reasoning.” The lesson is “do not promote effort without paired evidence.”

A useful follow-up experiment keeps the same X-axis but changes the workload slice: short factual, multi-step, tool-agent, and synthesis tasks should each earn their own effort setting. That routing policy is where reasoning usually pays off.

What this slice does and does not prove: it shows that on this short-factual workload shape, in one tenant and one region under this pricing snapshot, the effort ladder bought cost and hidden tokens without a matching quality lift. It does not prove the same shape holds for synthesis, multi-step, or tool-using work, or under a future pricing snapshot.

The practical rule

Keep short factual work at the lowest effort that already answers correctly, and promote a higher effort only after a quality gate moves on this workload shape — read next to its paired cost, latency, and token-composition charts.
